# Positioning Methodology — Full Reference

## Layer 1: Market frame

Three strategic options:

| Option | Definition | When to use |
|---|---|---|
| Category creation | Define a new category the product owns | Existing categories don't capture the value; buyers don't have language for the problem yet |
| Category redefinition | Reframe what an existing category means | Category exists but incumbents defined it narrowly; you can shift evaluation criteria |
| Category competition | Enter an established category and win on differentiation | Buyers are already educated; speed-to-market matters more than narrative control |

Old way vs. new way: name what changed (technology, scale, regulation, or buyer expectations) that makes the old approach insufficient *now*. For a renewable energy asset management product, common forcing functions: a portfolio crossing a scale threshold (10 sites to 60), a regulatory reporting requirement tightening (e.g., CNE compliance windows), or SCADA/monitoring tooling that wasn't built for multi-site, multi-technology (solar + wind + storage) fleets.

Output: category name, market evolution stage (emerging / growing / mature / disrupted), old-way-vs-new-way statement, and the forcing function.

## Layer 2: Target buyer and context

- Primary buyer profile: what situation are they in, what are they accountable for, what's the job to be done.
- Trigger events: what causes them to start evaluating right now (a missed production target, a new site coming online, a board asking for tighter OPEX).
- Constraints: budget cycle, procurement process, risk tolerance.
- Stage of awareness: aware of the problem only, aware of the solution category, or already comparing vendors.

## Layer 3: Core problem statement

- Primary problem, stated as the buyer would say it, not as engineering would.
- Secondary problems: downstream consequences that add urgency.
- Cost of inaction: what happens if nothing changes.
- Why current solutions fail — be specific. Not "existing tools are outdated" but "plant-level SCADA systems report faults locally; nothing rolls them up across a 40-site portfolio, so a technician has to notice a pattern that a dashboard should have caught three days earlier."

## Layer 4: Unique value and differentiation

- Primary value delivered: the single most important, ideally measurable, outcome.
- Secondary benefits.
- Rational appeal (ROI, risk reduction, avoided cost) and emotional appeal (confidence, control) — never lead with the latter in B2B, never ignore it either.
- Proof points: customer outcomes, third-party validation, technical differentiation, ecosystem advantages.
- The true "why us": what can't be easily copied — data advantage, workflow depth, integration breadth, category ownership.

## Layer 5: Competitive positioning

Direct competitor table:

| Competitor | Why buyers choose them | Why we win | Why we lose | Our counter-move |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Indirect alternatives — the "do nothing" option is always a competitor: manual monitoring, spreadsheets, legacy SCADA-only reporting, and why they persist (familiarity, sunk cost, procurement inertia).

Strategic whitespace: where every competitor is weak or silent.

## Output structure

1. Positioning statement (the template sentence).
2. Market frame (category, stage, old-vs-new, forcing function).
3. Target buyer and context.
4. Core problem statement.
5. Unique value proposition, with proof.
6. Competitive landscape and differentiation.
7. Messaging architecture: core pillar + 2-4 supporting pillars, each with a one-sentence claim, 2-3 proof points, and the persona it resonates with most.
8. Strategy implications: how this changes the sales opening, marketing themes, and roadmap commitments.

## Language guidelines

| Use | Avoid |
|---|---|
| Outcome verbs: achieve, eliminate, recover, guarantee | Vague terms: leverage, unlock, empower |
| Specific metrics when available (MWh recovered, uptime %, hours saved) | Unsupported superlatives: best-in-class, leading |
| The buyer's own language, pulled from interviews or reviews | Internal jargon and feature names |
| "You" framing | "We" framing in market-facing copy |
