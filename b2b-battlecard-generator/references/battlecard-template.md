# Battlecard Template — Full Reference

## 1. Competitive snapshot

- What they are: one-sentence positioning as buyers perceive it, not as the competitor's own marketing describes it.
- Who they typically win against: the account profile where they win most — segment, use case, buyer type.
- Where they're strongest: 2-3 genuine strengths, stated honestly.
- Where they're vulnerable: 2-3 consistent weaknesses, grounded in deal patterns or customer evidence.

## 2. Head-to-head positioning

Where we win decisively (3-5 bullets): "[our capability] vs. [their gap] -> [buyer outcome]." Example for a renewable-energy asset management sale: "portfolio-wide fault detection across mixed solar/wind/storage assets vs. their single-technology monitoring -> catches cross-site patterns their per-technology dashboards can't see."

Where they win (2-3 bullets, honest): the scenario where the competitor is the right call, and how to qualify out gracefully instead of fighting an unwinnable deal.

Framing rule: never "we're better" — always "different in ways that matter for this buyer."

## 3. Win themes

3-6 narratives anchored in outcomes, formatted as: "[theme name]": "[customer outcome statement]." Example: "Faster fault detection": "Customers typically catch a production-impacting fault within hours instead of the days it took with per-site SCADA alerts alone, because the platform correlates signals across the whole portfolio." Each theme should be phrased from the buyer's perspective, backed by a metric or case reference, and implicitly contrast with a competitor gap.

## 4. Discovery questions

6-10 questions covering: exposing a gap the buyer doesn't know to ask about, surfacing a hidden requirement that favors this product, reframing what "good" looks like in the category, testing implementation readiness on live operating assets, and uncovering who else is involved in the decision. Write them conversationally, the way a rep would actually ask.

## 5. Objections and rebuttals

For each of 4-6 major objections: the objection as the buyer says it, what they really mean underneath it, and a response that validates the concern before redirecting, under four sentences, ending with a question that moves the conversation forward.

## 6. Competitive landmines

- Pricing traps: where a direct price comparison hurts, and how to reframe toward total cost or avoided-loss framing instead.
- Feature-comparison traps: features that look better on a spec sheet but matter less in practice, and how to handle the demo-level comparison.
- Demo pitfalls: what not to show and why.
- Exaggerated claims to counter: competitor claims buyers take at face value, with the evidence-based counter. Never make an unverifiable claim about a competitor — frame sensitive points as "worth asking them about."

## 7. Sales talk track

30-second positioning answer for "how are you different from [competitor]?" — written as spoken language, not a tagline. Plus 2-3 verbatim call snippets: an opener that establishes competitive framing without sounding defensive, a response to "we're already evaluating [competitor]," and a late-stage reframe if the deal is leaning toward them. First person, contractions, human.

## 8. When you win / when you lose

You're likely to win when: 3-5 specific account-profile or evaluation-criteria signals. You're likely to lose when: 3-5 specific warning signs (e.g., "the champion goes quiet" is useful; "deal is at risk" is not). Late-stage recovery: what to ask the champion, what proof point to introduce, what executive move to make.

## 9. Cheat sheet

Three things to always say. Three things to never say. Three questions to always ask. Printable, scannable in ten seconds — this is what a rep reads right before the call.

## Style rules

Write like a top-performing seller: direct, confident, specific. Delete "innovative," "leading," "cutting-edge," and "best-in-class" on sight. Short paragraphs, tight bullets. Be honest about weaknesses — a rep who knows where they're weak is stronger than one who doesn't.
